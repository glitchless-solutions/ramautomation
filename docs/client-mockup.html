Questions we get weekly

Before you send the RFQ

What actually drives the price of a control panel?

Components, almost always. The enclosure, drives, PLC, and safety devices typically make up the majority of the cost, and labor is a smaller share than most buyers expect. That's why we quote against a fixed component list — so you can see what's driving the number and decide whether a different drive or enclosure is worth the change.

Can you apply the UL label, or do I need a field evaluation?

We apply it. As a UL 508A listed shop we build under our own file and the panel ships with the label already on it. Field evaluations are what you need when a panel was built by someone who isn't listed, and they typically add cost and schedule per panel.

What is SCCR and why does my inspector care about it?

Short circuit current rating is the maximum fault current the panel can safely withstand. It has to be marked on the panel and it has to be at least as high as the available fault current at the point of installation. It's one of the most common reasons a panel gets red-tagged, so we calculate it during design rather than discovering it at startup.

Do you program the panel, or just build it?

Either. We have software engineers on staff, so we can deliver the panel with the PLC, HMI, and drive code written, tested on the bench, and commissioned on your floor. Plenty of customers do their own programming and just want the hardware — that's fine too, and it's quoted separately so you're not paying for engineering you don't need.

Which industrial networks can you work with?

EtherCAT, EtherNet/IP, PROFINET, PROFIBUS, and Modbus TCP and RTU, including mixed architectures where a machine has to talk to equipment on more than one protocol. If your plant standard is one network and a piece of vendor equipment arrives on another, that's a routine problem here rather than a change order.

Do I own the PLC program?

Yes. Source code is delivered unlocked and documented along with schematics, CAD files, and the operator manual. You are not required to come back to us to make a change — though we're happy to be the ones who do it.

Will you build from drawings another shop produced?

Yes, that's Path A. We'll review the package first and tell you if we see anything that won't pass UL or won't work as drawn, but we don't require you to pay us to redraw someone else's design.

Qualify it early

Custom automation is not always the answer

A custom machine is a capital project. Before anyone writes a spec, it's worth being honest about whether the numbers work — so here's the test we walk customers through on the first call, including the cases where we tell people not to do it.

Usually worth it when

The case is strong

  • The part runs at volume and will keep running for 3+ years
  • The operation is repetitive, and quality varies operator to operator
  • You can't hire for the role, or turnover keeps resetting your training
  • The task is ergonomically hard, hot, or otherwise a safety exposure
  • Scrap or rework on this step is measurable and annoying
  • 100% inspection is required and humans can't keep up
Usually not worth it when

Say so early

  • The product changes design every few months
  • Annual volume is low and unlikely to grow
  • The upstream process isn't stable yet — automate chaos and you get fast chaos
  • A fixture, a jig, or a bench-top tool solves 80% of it for 5% of the cost
  • The real bottleneck is somewhere else on the line

If you're in this column, we'll say so. We'd rather build you a $6,000 fixture that works than a $300,000 machine you regret.

Questions we get weekly

Before you spec a machine

How do I justify this to whoever signs the check?

Usually on labor, scrap, and throughput — in that order. During the study stage we build the payback math with your real numbers: current cycle time, operators on the task, scrap rate, and the fully loaded labor cost. If the payback lands past about 24 months, that's worth knowing before you spend anything, and we'll say so.

What happens if my part design changes later?

Ask us this during design, not after. If you know a change is coming, we can build in adjustability, quick-change tooling, or extra station capacity for a fraction of what it costs to retrofit later. Machines get expensive when flexibility is added after the fact.

Who handles the safety and risk assessment?

We do, as part of the design. Guarding, light curtains, interlocks, and the safety-rated control architecture are designed in from the start and documented — not bolted on after your EHS team sees the machine for the first time.

Do you work from our concept, or start from scratch?

Either. Plenty of customers arrive with a sketch and a cycle time target, and plenty arrive with a part and a problem. We'll tell you honestly which parts of an existing concept we think will work and which we'd change.

Can you take over a machine another integrator started?

Sometimes. It depends on the state of the design, the quality of the documentation, and how far the build got. We'll look at it and give you a straight answer, including if the honest answer is that starting over is cheaper.

Questions we get weekly

Before you send the drawing

What does "IPC/WHMA-A-620 certified" actually mean?

It means our operators and inspectors have been trained and certified against the standard, and that we build and inspect to its published acceptance criteria rather than to in-house opinion. The standard was developed by IPC and the Wire Harness Manufacturers Association and is the industry consensus document for cable and harness assemblies. Certification is maintained through recertification, not earned once.

I don't have a drawing. Can you still quote?

Usually. Send the harness you're replacing, or a wire list with connector part numbers and lengths, and we can work from that. For anything going into production we'll produce a controlled drawing so future lots are built identically — you own that drawing.

Do you have a minimum order quantity?

No. We build single prototypes, and we'd rather you validate one before ordering a thousand. Per-unit price does drop meaningfully at volume, and we'll show you the price break points in the quote.

Why is another quote so much cheaper?

Nine times out of ten it's one of three things: a lower IPC class assumed, a cheaper connector or wire substituted, or the work is being built offshore. None of those are automatically wrong — but they should be a decision you made, not a surprise you discovered. Our quotes state the class and the exact BOM so you can compare like for like.

Do you test every harness?

Yes. 100% continuity test on every unit, with hipot where the application calls for it, and the test report ships with the lot. Crimps are pull-tested to the standard, and records are retained.

Architecture

When the machine is too long to bring to us

Plenty of the equipment we retrofit can't be crated and shipped — it's 20, 40, 60 feet of line bolted to a plant floor. That changes the electrical architecture, not just the logistics.

The old way

One central panel

Every sensor, valve, and motor home-runs back to a single enclosure at one end of the machine.

What that costs you

  • Hundreds of long wire runs end to end
  • Large conduit and cable tray fills
  • Days of on-site pulling and terminating
  • A fault at one end means walking to the other to troubleshoot
  • Adding a device later means a new run the full length
How we build it

Distributed I/O over Ethernet

Small I/O nodes placed local to the devices they serve, linked back to the controller on a single industrial Ethernet run.

What changes

  • Field wiring drops to short local runs
  • One network cable replaces the bundle down the machine
  • Install time and conduit cost fall sharply
  • Node-level diagnostics — the network tells you which section faulted
  • Adding a device later means wiring to the nearest node
  • Sections can be commissioned independently during cutover

EtherNet/IP, EtherCAT, PROFINET, or your existing plant standard. Architecture is decided during the survey, before the quote.

Before you replace the machine

Should I retrofit or just buy a new machine?

It depends almost entirely on the mechanical condition. If the frame, drives, and tooling are sound and the machine still makes good parts, a retrofit typically runs a fraction of replacement cost, keeps a footprint your plant is already laid out for, and doesn't require retraining operators on new mechanics. If the mechanics are worn out or the process itself needs to change, a retrofit is money spent on a machine that will still be limited afterward — and we'll tell you that during the survey.

We have no drawings for this machine. Can you still do it?

Yes, and it's extremely common. Part of the survey is reverse engineering what's actually installed rather than what someone thinks is installed. You end up with a complete AutoCAD package — frequently the first accurate documentation the machine has ever had, which is worth something on its own the next time anything goes wrong.

How long will my line actually be down?

For most retrofits, one planned weekend or maintenance window. That's achievable because the panel is built and the code is written and simulated before anyone touches your machine. We commit to a downtime window in the quote rather than discovering it during the shutdown — and if a machine can't be done in a weekend, you'll hear that before you sign, not after.

Can you write recipes for different products?

Yes, and it's usually one of the biggest practical wins of a retrofit. Instead of an operator dialing in settings from memory or a laminated sheet, product parameters get stored, named, version-controlled, and recalled from the HMI. Changeover gets faster and far more repeatable, and it removes a common source of scrap.

Do you do the field wiring, or do we hire an electrician?

We do it. Our technicians handle on-site wiring, integration, and startup, which matters more than it sounds — when the people who designed the panel are the ones landing the wires, problems get solved on the spot instead of turning into a scheduling conversation between two vendors.

What if we already built the machine ourselves?

That's Path B, and it's a large share of our work. Send us the mechanical system and a description of how it should behave, and we handle the electrical design, panel, programming, recipes, and field integration. You don't need a written spec to start — a walkthrough of the machine is usually enough.
